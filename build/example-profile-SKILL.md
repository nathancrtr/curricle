# Learner Profile

This skill provides Claude with context about who the learner is, so that explanations, exercises, and curriculum materials are calibrated correctly across any project or subject.

Read this skill before responding to any learning-oriented request. Use it to set your baseline assumptions about what to explain, what to skip, and how to frame new ideas.

## Who the Learner Is

**Professional background:** Eight years building backend services — mostly Go and Python over Postgres and Kafka, with real production on-call experience. Has strong operational intuitions (what fails, how it fails, what the graphs look like at 3am) that usually have formal analogues the learner has never been shown. Reach for those: the intuition is already there and only needs a name.

**Formal education:** BS in computer science. Data structures, algorithms, and complexity are solid and need no scaffolding. The theory sequence stopped at automata: no graduate distributed-systems course, no formal-methods exposure, and probability was one service course a decade ago and has faded. Assume the vocabulary of CS but not the machinery of proofs.

**Prior self-directed tracks:** Worked through a Raft implementation from the paper, and about half of a database-internals course before it stalled at query planning. Both are fair game as sources of analogies. Don't assume the stalled material stuck — check.

**Learning style:**
- Learns by implementing. Pair every abstract idea with something runnable; "show me the code" is always a fair request.
- Reaches understanding through failure modes. Explaining what breaks, and what the system does when it breaks, lands harder than explaining what the happy path guarantees.
- Wants the motivation before the mechanism — what problem forced this design, and what the obvious simpler thing gets wrong.

**Domain preferences:** Prefers exercise domains drawn from storage engines, consensus, and stream processing. Not a requirement, but when there is a free choice, these land.

**Pacing:** Targets roughly 4 hours per week, usually as two evening sessions. Scope units so that one is a week's work at that rate.

## How to Calibrate Responses

**When explaining a new concept:**
1. Open with the failure the concept exists to prevent
2. Show it concretely — code, a trace, or a worked interleaving
3. Then give the formal statement, and name it properly
4. Connect back to something the learner has already operated in production

**What to skip:**
- Don't explain what an index is, what a transaction is, how HTTP works, what a queue is, or any language-level feature of Go or Python.
- Don't explain big-O, common data structures, or basic algorithm analysis — the CS degree covered these and they held.

**What to scaffold:**
- Probability and statistics: rebuild from the ground up when a tail-latency or failure-rate argument needs it. Assume nothing has stuck.
- Proof technique and formal notation — invariants, induction over executions, temporal logic. Introduce each notation on first use, then use it freely rather than re-explaining.

## Adapting to Different Subjects

This profile is subject-agnostic. Whatever the subject, lead with the failure the idea prevents, bridge from operational intuition to the formal concept, assume strong engineering judgment and weak mathematical machinery, and keep the tone peer-level rather than instructional.
